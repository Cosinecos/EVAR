The Blue Counter

The incident took place in a campus newspaper office on election night. Phones rang over the clatter of keyboards and the smell of cheap pizza. During the event, the note vanished from the editor's locked drawer. The missing item, an anonymous source note, had been checked before the room became crowded, and no one was allowed to remove it openly. Staff first described the problem as confusion caused by the crowd, but the scene contained details that made accidental loss unlikely.

The suspect list was deliberately small: Keira Frost the campaign volunteer, Cyrus Hayes the layout designer, Ivy Reed the managing editor. The case could be solved by linking a short time window with one specialized access clue and one physical trace.

Keira Frost, the campaign volunteer, was noticed by two witnesses and seemed nervous, yet the timing of that behavior did not match the final disappearance. Cyrus Hayes, the layout designer, had a personal irritation that sounded like motive, but it did not account for the physical trace. Ivy Reed, the managing editor, gave a calm statement. They did not deny being close to the relevant equipment, only that their presence was ordinary for their job.

The first material clue was thin paper strips under the layout desk. It was not located on the normal visitor path; it appeared close to a staff-side area where a casual visitor would have had little reason to stand. The second material clue was a layout knife with paper fibers on the blade. It should not have been involved after the final check unless someone used it during the disturbance. The third constraint was temporal: the office printer jammed during the candidate-call rush. This narrowed the possible window and ruled out explanations that required a long, unobserved search.

Initially, Keira Frost drew most of the attention. They were connected with a campaign volunteer hovering near the office, and several witnesses remembered their mood before the disappearance. However, the timeline weakened that theory: the troubling behavior happened before an anonymous source note was last confirmed present. That made the behavior suspicious but not causally sufficient.

Suspicion then shifted to Cyrus Hayes, who was associated with a reporter angry about edits. This detail created a plausible story of opportunity, but it did not explain thin paper strips under the layout desk or the use of a hollow ruler tube beside the layout desk. A visible opportunity was not enough unless it also matched the hidden mechanism.

The hiding method was the strongest clue. The responsible person did not need to carry an anonymous source note out through the main exit. The item could instead be placed in a hollow ruler tube beside the layout desk, a location ordinary enough to survive the first search. This mattered because only the managing editor knew the drawer could be opened by lifting the back rail. Someone without that knowledge might have acted impulsively, but would not naturally choose that container.

The access pattern pointed in the same direction: the managing editor handled the drawer key after the printer jam. The motive also matched that role. The disappearance would help someone who wanted to prevent publication of a correction that would damage a favored candidate. Other suspects had embarrassment, anger, or opportunity, but they failed to satisfy the complete chain of timing, access, physical trace, container knowledge, and motive.

By the end of the inquiry, the question was no longer who had a broad chance to be nearby. Many people had that. The question was who matched the complete evidence chain: access during the short window, knowledge of the hidden container, connection to the physical trace, and a motive that required delayed discovery.

Question: Who was responsible for the disappearance of an anonymous source note, and what evidence supports the conclusion?

The investigators treated each statement as a constraint rather than as a confession. A claim was useful only if it explained when the act happened, how the object moved, and why the first search failed.

The order of observations was also important. A suspicious act before the final verification could not by itself explain a disappearance afterward, even if it made a person look guilty.

The physical trace was small, but it linked the scene to a particular mechanism. Without that mechanism, the case would reduce to guesswork among people with ordinary access.

The hidden container changed the logic of the case. The responsible person needed confidence that the object would remain unseen during a quick search and could be recovered later.

The motive had to fit delay. A person who simply wanted the object immediately would have taken a different risk, while the actual plan depended on the object staying nearby but unnoticed.
